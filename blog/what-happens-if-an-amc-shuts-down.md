People evaluate funds on returns. Almost nobody asks the more basic question first: if the company managing this fund runs into trouble, what happens to what I put in?

It is a fair question, and the structure of a mutual fund is designed around it.

## Three parties, not one

A mutual fund in Bangladesh separates responsibilities across separate entities rather than concentrating them in the fund manager.

The asset management company decides what the fund buys and sells. The trustee oversees whether the manager is operating within the fund's stated mandate and the applicable rules. The custodian holds the fund's assets and records its transactions.

The separation is the protection. The party choosing what to buy is not the party holding the assets, so the manager instructs rather than possesses. Fund assets sit with the custodian and are not the manager's own property to spend or pledge.

This structure is not a courtesy any individual manager extends. It is how authorised funds are constituted, and every fund's prospectus names the specific trustee and custodian appointed to it.

## Why that differs from a bank deposit

The comparison is worth making because a deposit is the more familiar reference point.

Money in a bank sits on the bank's own balance sheet. The bank owes it to you, and if the bank fails, you are a creditor whose recovery depends on what the bank has left and whatever protection framework applies.

Fund assets are not structured that way. They were never merged into the manager's own assets, so the manager's financial position does not, by itself, put the portfolio at risk. The trustee's role in a disruption is continuity of the fund rather than winding it up.

## What this does not protect you from

Ordinary investment losses, which are the far more likely thing to happen.

If the fund's holdings fall in value, that loss is real and it belongs to the fund's investors. No structural safeguard changes it. What the separation of roles addresses is a narrower risk: that assets go missing, get misused, or become unaccounted for because of what happened to the company managing them.

Confusing the two is common, and it leads people to treat governance protections as a guarantee about returns. They are not related.

## What to actually check

Before investing, it is entirely reasonable to look up who a fund's trustee and custodian are, and to confirm they are independent, authorised entities rather than parts of the same group as the manager. That information is in the prospectus.

If a fund cannot answer that question clearly, treat the difficulty itself as informative.

## A note on sources

This describes the general structure of authorised open-end mutual funds and how the roles divide. It is not legal advice, and specific obligations and procedures are set by BSEC's rules and by each fund's own trust deed and prospectus. Those documents govern, not this page, and they are where anything that matters to your decision should be confirmed.

:::faq
Q: Who actually holds a mutual fund's assets?
A: The custodian appointed to the fund, which is a separate authorised entity from the asset management company that makes investment decisions.

Q: What does the trustee do?
A: It oversees whether the asset manager is operating within the fund's stated mandate and the applicable rules, and it is the party responsible for the fund's continuity if the manager cannot continue.

Q: Does this mean my investment cannot lose value?
A: No. These arrangements address governance and custody risk. Market losses are entirely possible and are borne by the fund's investors.

Q: Where can I confirm any of this for a specific fund?
A: In that fund's prospectus and trust deed, which name its trustee and custodian, and in BSEC's own published records.
:::
